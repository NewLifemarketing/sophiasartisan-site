Chocolate conching

What chocolate conching does to particles, viscosity and volatile compounds

Conching is the long, heated mixing step between refining and tempering. This is what it changes in the material: how the solid particles meet the fat, how much water remains, which volatile compounds leave, and how the mass flows.

By the time chocolate reaches the conche, its particles are already about as small as they will get. Refining has done that work. What chocolate conching changes is everything around the particles: how completely the fat covers them, how much water is left between them, which volatile compounds remain in the mass, and how the whole suspension flows. It is the step where flake or powder becomes a liquid that can be tempered and moulded.

Where conching sits in the process

A 2004 review in the European Journal of Lipid Science and Technology lists five basic steps shared by every chocolate process: mixing, refining, conching, tempering and cooling. Refining reduces sugar and cocoa solids to a particle size below about 30 µm, normally on two- and five-roll refiners, according to a 2007 review in Trends in Food Science & Technology. The same review notes that refiners also break up agglomerates and coat each freshly fractured surface with lipid.

Chocolate conching follows. The 2007 review describes it as agitating the chocolate above 50 °C for some hours. A 2008 review in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition describes it as generally a two-stage process. In the first stage, mechanical work and heat turn flake or powder into a paste, removing moisture and unwanted volatile compounds and distributing the lipids through a continuous fat phase. In the second, added cocoa butter and lecithin turn the thick paste into a free-flowing liquid.

Time and temperature trade against each other, and higher temperatures shorten the process. The published conditions are 10 to 16 hours at 49 to 52 °C for milk crumb chocolate, 16 to 24 hours at up to 60 °C for chocolate made with milk powder, and 70 °C and up to 82 °C for dark chocolate.

What chocolate conching does to particles and viscosity

Chocolate is a suspension: solid particles of sugar and cocoa, and of milk solids where present, held in a continuous fat phase. The 2007 review puts the solids at 65 to 75% of the mass. With that much solid matter, how the chocolate flows depends heavily on the particles' surfaces. Smaller particles raise both the plastic viscosity and the yield value, because more surface area is in contact with cocoa butter. Refining creates that surface. Chocolate conching has to cover it.

A 2019 review in Molecules lists coating the solid particles with fat among the purposes of conching, alongside evaporating volatile acids, removing excess moisture and reaching the proper viscosity. The 2008 review adds that angular sugar crystals are modified in the process.

Chocolate flow is usually described with two numbers, and conching moves both. The yield value is the force needed to start the mass flowing; the 2007 review relates it to the forces between solid particles. The plastic viscosity is the resistance once it flows, and it depends mostly on the free fat available to lubricate the particles. That review reports that adding 1% fat to a chocolate at 28% fat almost halves its plastic viscosity, while above 32% further fat changes very little.

Water

Water is the clearest case. Molten chocolate typically holds 0.5 to 1.5% moisture, mostly inside the cocoa solids, and at that level flow is unaffected. More water aggregates sugar particles and adds friction at their surfaces. At 3 to 4% water, viscosity and yield value rise markedly. The 2007 review gives the commercial arithmetic: for every 0.3% of extra moisture left at the end of chocolate conching, about 1% more fat has to be added to bring the viscosity back.

Viscosity modifiers

The last stage is also where viscosity is adjusted deliberately. The 2007 review reports that lecithin changes both flow values sharply, but above about 0.5% the yield value starts to rise again while the plastic viscosity keeps falling. Another surface-active ingredient, PGPR, can cut the yield value by half at 0.2%. The literature describes combining the two to balance one value against the other.

What leaves the mass: volatile compounds

The food science literature describes this part of conching mostly in terms of flavour. As a process step it is mass transfer and chemistry, and it can be measured. The 2008 review notes that the air around a working conche carries acetic acid, a fermentation product, and that quantitative studies have confirmed an early loss of short-chain volatile fatty acids. Volatile phenols fall by about 80% in headspace concentration within a few hours of conching, a figure the 2019 review also reports for volatile polyphenols.

Not everything leaves, and not everything that remains was there at the start. The 2008 review describes Strecker aldehydes being partly lost through evaporation or reaction, while at least one compound, 2-phenyl-5-methyl-2-hexenal, increases through an aldol condensation of two smaller aldehydes followed by dehydration. Chocolate conching is a reactor as well as an evaporator.

Why the step matters downstream

Conching sets the state of the fat phase that every later step has to work with. Tempering, explained in tempering milk chocolate, depends on that fat phase crystallising correctly. In a cannabis chocolate, the extract has to be evenly dispersed through the same fat phase before the batch is moulded, because once chocolate sets, whatever distribution existed in the liquid is locked in. The reasoning behind building the extract into the cocoa butter is set out on our formulation science page.

What can be confirmed afterwards is the result in the set piece, not the conditions in the conche. Every lot we make is laboratory tested.

FAQ

What does conching actually do to chocolate?

It agitates the chocolate above 50 °C for some hours, turning flake or powder into a liquid that can be tempered and moulded. Along the way it coats the particles with fat, removes moisture and volatile acids, and brings the mass to the proper viscosity.

How long does conching take?

Time and temperature trade against each other. The published conditions are 10 to 16 hours at 49 to 52 °C for milk crumb chocolate, 16 to 24 hours at up to 60 °C for milk powder chocolate, and 70 °C up to 82 °C for dark chocolate.

Why does leftover moisture matter after conching?

Extra water aggregates sugar particles and adds friction at their surfaces, raising viscosity and yield value. The 2007 review reports that every 0.3% of extra moisture left at the end needs about 1% more fat to bring the viscosity back.

Is conching the same as refining?

No. Refining reduces sugar and cocoa solids to below about 30 µm, so the particles are already about as small as they will get. Conching changes everything around them, from fat coverage and water to volatile compounds and flow.
