Dose uniformity in edibles, and how a processor checks it

A package of ten pieces makes ten separate claims. This is what each piece has to get right, what the Cannabis Regulations hold it to, and where in production uniformity is checked.

A package of ten pieces, each declaring 1 mg of THC, makes ten claims rather than one. The label does not say the package averages 1 mg a piece. It says each piece carries it. Dose uniformity in edibles is the manufacturing problem of making all ten of those claims true at once, and then showing that they are.

Why the problem gets harder as the number gets smaller is covered in the engineering behind a one-milligram dose. This post is about the other half: what uniformity is made of, and where a processor checks for it.

What the Regulations ask of every piece

Two provisions in the Cannabis Regulations carry the requirement. Section 97(2) holds edible cannabis to a permitted band around any quantity of THC or CBD displayed on its label. Section 98.1 prohibits a licence holder from selling edible cannabis whose immediate container holds multiple discrete units unless the properties of each unit, including size but excluding flavour and colour, are consistent. The bands, and how they widen as the declared figure falls, are set out in the Health Canada cannabinoids rules for edible cannabis.

Read together, the two sections mean a package cannot pass on its average. A run in which half the pieces sat well above the declared figure and half well below could produce a batch mean exactly on target and still fail, because the per-unit figure on the label is a statement about each unit, and section 98.1 asks the units to match each other.

Dose is two numbers multiplied

The cannabinoid in a single moulded piece is the product of two quantities: the concentration of cannabinoid in the chocolate, and the mass of chocolate in the piece. A piece holds the declared amount only if both are right. That gives dose uniformity in edibles two separate ways to fail, and each needs its own control.

Concentration: the batch has to be even

If the cannabinoid is not evenly distributed through the batch, pieces moulded from different parts of it will differ even when every piece weighs exactly the same. A batch can look fully mixed and still be uneven at the scale of a single cavity, and a moulding cavity is a small scale. Once chocolate sets, whatever distribution existed in the liquid is fixed in the piece. There is no correcting it afterwards.

That is why we build the cannabinoid into the cocoa butter phase as a controlled step of its own, rather than adding it to finished chocolate. Our formulation science page sets out the reasoning. It adds a manufacturing step in order to remove a source of variability.

Evenness also has to hold over time. A batch is not moulded in an instant. If the first cavities filled and the last are to carry the same amount, the batch has to be as uniform at the end of the run as it was at the start, so uniformity is a property of the whole run as well as the whole batch.

Mass: every cavity has to hold the same amount

The second number is the one most often overlooked. A perfectly even batch still produces uneven pieces if the cavities are filled unevenly, because a piece carrying ten per cent more chocolate carries ten per cent more cannabinoid. Fill weight is a dose control, not only a packaging one.

It is also why section 98.1 names size. Consistent dimensions and consistent mass are part of the same requirement, and the Regulations write it into the lot record as well: section 225 requires a processor to keep, for each lot of edible cannabis put into discrete units, the net weight of each unit and the number of units.

How a processor checks dose uniformity in edibles

Checking happens at three points, and only the last of them is laboratory analysis.

  • The method, before it is used. Under section 88(1)(c), the methods and procedures used to produce, sample and test cannabis must be approved by the quality assurance person before they are put into practice. A mixing step, a depositing set-up and a sampling plan are all methods in that sense.
  • The line, while it runs. Unit mass can be checked during production, which is where a drifting fill is caught and corrected rather than discovered after the lot is finished. We work in small batches for this reason: each production run is small enough to be checked closely and adjusted.
  • The lot, once it is complete. Section 90 requires cannabinoid testing on each lot or batch, conducted on the cannabis in its final form. For a chocolate, that means set pieces. Section 92 requires that testing to use validated methods on a representative sample of each lot.

The word representative carries most of the weight. For a uniformity question, pieces drawn from one corner of one tray describe that corner. A sample that represents the lot has to reach across it: different positions in the mould, and the beginning, middle and end of the run. Section 92 does not prescribe how the sample is drawn. It requires that the sample represent the lot, and leaves the processor to show that it does. The same section requires a portion of that sample to be retained for at least one year after the last sale of any part of the lot, in a quantity large enough to determine the cannabinoid content again.

We hold every lot to that sequence. Cannabinoid inputs are sourced from licensed suppliers and verified on receipt, every lot is laboratory tested, and a quality assurance person releases a lot only after the results are in. Product does not ship on commercial judgement alone.

Why the average is not the answer

Uneven distribution does not average out. It produces pieces above the declared figure and pieces below it, and nothing on a package tells anyone which kind a given piece is. That is the reason an edible label carries a per-unit figure at all, and the reason uniformity has to be engineered into the batch, the cavity and the run rather than inferred from a mean.

What a lot result can show is narrow and exact: that pieces drawn from across the lot, measured by a validated method, sat within the band the Regulations set around the declared figure. That is the evidence a per-unit number on a legal edible stands on.
