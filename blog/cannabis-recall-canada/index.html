Cannabis recall Canada: who decides, how it is classed, how notice travels

A recall starts as a document sent to Health Canada before any store is told. This is the sequence the Cannabis Act and Regulations set, the three recall types, and the route a notice takes to an Ontario shelf.

A recall is not a news story first. In the voluntary cannabis recall Canada's Cannabis Regulations describe, the first step is a document sent to Health Canada before a single store is told, and the last is a written report filed within 30 days of the recall being completed. Between those two points sits a sequence fixed in federal law and, in Ontario, in the provincial distributor's own procedure.

Who decides

There are two routes, and they start in different places.

The first is voluntary. A licence holder that identifies a problem with a product it has sold or distributed decides to recall it. Health Canada's cannabis voluntary recall guide encourages the most readily identifiable licence holder in the supply chain to take that responsibility, which is typically the one that owns the brand or whose information appears on the label. The guide is explicit that the obligations apply to every licence holder in the chain, and that a recall is a collaborative process among them.

The second is ordered. Section 76 of the Cannabis Act allows the Minister, on reasonable grounds that a recall is necessary to protect public health or public safety, to order a person that sells or distributes the cannabis to recall it. The order must give its reasons and specify the time and manner of the recall. If it is not complied with, section 77 allows the Minister to carry out the recall at that person's expense, and section 35 prohibits selling or distributing cannabis that is the subject of a recall order.

Either way, the machinery has to exist before it is needed. Section 46 of the Cannabis Regulations requires a licence holder, other than an analytical testing laboratory, to maintain a system of control that permits the rapid and complete recall of every lot or batch it has sold or distributed. It must also run a recall simulation at least once every 12 months, write up how it was conducted and what it found, and keep that document for two years.

What Health Canada is told, and when

Under section 247, a licence holder must give the Minister a document before it commences a voluntary recall of a product sold or distributed in Canada. The contents are specific:

  • a description of the product, including the brand name, and the number of each lot or batch to be recalled
  • the reasons for the recall
  • the quantity sold or distributed in Canada, the number of persons it went to, and the period over which it was sold
  • how and when the recall will be carried out, how the Minister will be kept informed, and when it is expected to be complete
  • contact information for a representative responsible for the recall

Within 72 hours of that document, the holder must add an evaluation of the risk. Within 30 days of the recall being completed, it must file a written report setting out the results and the measures taken to prevent a recurrence.

The three recall types

Health Canada assigns each recall a type, based on what the licence holder reports, and the type sets the pace. The guide defines them this way:

  • Type I: there is a reasonable probability that use of or exposure to the product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death. The guide's illustration is a lot of edible cannabis with E. coli.
  • Type II: use or exposure may cause temporary adverse health consequences, or the probability of serious adverse health consequences is remote. The illustration is an extract labelled with a lower THC concentration than the product contained.
  • Type III: use or exposure is not likely to cause any adverse health consequences. The illustration is a run of labels printed without the mandatory health warning.

The type then sets the timetable. The guide suggests first contact with supply chain customers within one business day of commencing a Type I recall, within four for Type II and within seven for Type III. Follow-up scales the same way. For a Type I recall, the guide says, there should be no non-responders at all.

How notice reaches stores and the public

Every cannabis recall Canada runs then moves along the same chain the product did, in reverse. The recalling licence holder notifies its supply chain customers, and in Ontario the customer is the provincial distributor. The OCS Wholesale handbook for authorized retailers sets out what happens next. The OCS issues a product recall notice to each authorized retailer by email and posts it on OCSwholesale.ca and OCS.ca. The retailer must then:

  • segregate the recalled product in secure storage away from customers, clearly marked as not for sale, in line with the AGCO Registrar's Standards
  • post the recall notice where customers can see it, and keep it posted for 30 days
  • report, within 24 hours, how many recalled units are on hand, how many were sold before the recall, and how many had been returned by customers

A retailer's obligation does not wait for that email to be read. Section 5.3 of the Regulations prohibits a provincially authorized seller from selling a product it knows is the subject of a voluntary recall commenced for reasons of quality, or because the good production practice or product requirements are not being met. How product reaches an Ontario store in the first place is covered in how our product reaches an Ontario shelf.

The public notice is Health Canada's. Based on the licence holder's report, Health Canada publishes a notice on its Recalls and Safety Alerts website identifying the issue, the risk type and any action consumers should take. Cannabis recalls are listed there as consumer product recalls and can be found by searching for cannabis; recent notices have covered labelling errors and undeclared allergens. That database, not a press release or a social post, is where a cannabis recall Canada has announced is officially recorded.

What a recall depends on

A recall can only be as precise as the records behind it. It is scoped by lot, not by product name, which is why a lot number is required on every legal package. The rapid and complete cannabis recall Canada's Regulations require is possible only if a lot can be traced back to what went into it and forward to where it went. For edible cannabis, section 225 requires the processor to record, for each lot, the lot number of the cannabis used to produce it.

That is the reason the paperwork starts at the receiving door. Cannabinoid inputs and food ingredients arrive under the record-keeping the licence requires, every lot is laboratory tested, and a quality assurance person releases it before it ships. The sequence is set out on our craft manufacturing page. A recall system is tested once a year by simulation so that, if it is ever used, the lot it names can be found everywhere it went.
