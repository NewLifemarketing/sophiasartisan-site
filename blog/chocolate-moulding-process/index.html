The chocolate moulding process: fill, vibrate, cool, demould

Tempering gets the fat into the right crystal form. Moulding turns that liquid into a fixed number of solid units of a fixed size. Four stages, each with its own failure.

A moulded piece of chocolate is decided in the minutes between the depositor and the end of the cooling tunnel. Tempering, covered in tempering milk chocolate, gets the fat into the right crystal form. The chocolate moulding process is what turns that tempered liquid into a fixed number of solid units, each of a fixed size, each of which then carries its own declared figure.

The chocolate moulding process runs in four stages: filling, vibrating, cooling and demoulding. Each has a characteristic way of going wrong, and every one of those failures ends up inside the piece.

Filling: a measured amount into every cavity

A mould is a tray of identical cavities. On an industrial line, tempered chocolate is deposited into the individual cavities of a mould tray, which is how the background of a 2010 United States patent on moulding methods describes conventional practice (US 7,819,053).

Two things have to be right at the moment the chocolate reaches the mould. The first is the amount. Each cavity has to receive the same quantity, because the piece will contain exactly what went into it and nothing else. For a product carrying a cannabinoid, that makes filling part of the dose rather than only part of the shape.

The second is the state of the chocolate. It has to arrive still in temper, with its seed crystals intact, and fluid enough to reach every corner of the cavity. The room matters as much as the machine. Section 88.2 of the Cannabis Regulations requires a processor to keep temperature and humidity at levels appropriate for the activity being conducted, and on our line the batch is tempered and moulded into individual units under controlled temperature and humidity.

Vibrating: getting the air out

Deposited chocolate can carry trapped air. Left in, it becomes a defect. The same patent lists macrobubbles, microbubbles and craters as defects attributed to air in the chocolate, and notes that any one of them on a piece may be reason enough to reject it in quality control.

The conventional answer is vibration. The mould trays are vibrated so that the chocolate settles into and fills the cavity, and the movement works trapped air toward the surface. It is not a free fix. The patent records the trade-off plainly: macrobubbles may themselves be caused by air being forced upward toward the exposed surface as the tray is vibrated. How long and how hard a tray is vibrated is a setting that has to be developed, not assumed.

In a dosed product, air is more than a surface problem. A void inside a piece is chocolate that is not there. Two pieces with identical outer dimensions can differ in mass if one of them holds an air pocket, and the cannabinoid follows the mass, not the outline. Vibration is the step that makes a piece's volume and its mass describe the same thing.

Cooling: where the structure is fixed

Once filled and levelled, the trays pass through a cooling tunnel, where, as the patent puts it, the pieces set and harden. Cooling is not a separate step from tempering so much as its conclusion. The seed crystals formed during tempering template the rest of the fat as it solidifies, and the tunnel has to give the fat enough time to finish doing that before the piece is handled. A blend that crystallises more slowly needs a longer dwell; the milk fat case is set out in the tempering post linked above.

Cooling is also the point at which the dose stops being adjustable. Whatever distribution of cannabinoid existed in the liquid when it was deposited is locked in when the piece sets. There is no mixing a piece after the fact. That is why the distribution problem has to be solved upstream, in the cocoa butter phase, before the chocolate ever reaches a mould. Our formulation science page explains why the extract goes in there and not later.

Demoulding: why tempered chocolate lets go

Well-tempered chocolate comes out of its mould because it contracts as it solidifies. A 2011 review of industrial tempering lists contraction among the characteristics of Form V, the crystal form tempering aims for. Work presented at a 2000 food engineering conference of the International Institute of Refrigeration measured the height of chocolate as it solidified and found the method could clearly distinguish tempered from untempered material, noting that untempered chocolate does not separate easily from the mould.

That makes demoulding a test of every stage before it. At the end of the line the set pieces are removed from the tray. A tray that releases cleanly is evidence that the chocolate set in temper. A tray that holds on is evidence that it did not, and a piece that leaves part of itself behind in the cavity is a piece that has lost mass along with everything that mass contained.

What the chocolate moulding process delivers

Read as a whole, the chocolate moulding process is a sequence of controls on one variable: how much material ends up in each unit, and in what state. Filling sets the quantity. Vibration removes the air that would make quantity and size disagree. Cooling fixes the crystal structure and, with it, the distribution of everything in the fat. Demoulding shows whether all of that worked, before any laboratory does.

The laboratory still has the last word. Formulation, tempering and moulding sit in the same building in Thunder Bay, Ontario, and a sample from each lot goes to a licensed analytical laboratory before a quality assurance person releases it. The mould decides what each piece is. The analysis confirms it.
