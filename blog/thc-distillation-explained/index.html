Distillation THC: how a crude oil becomes distillate

Distillate is a word used every day in edible manufacturing and rarely explained outside it. Here is what the process does, what it cannot do, and why a measured input matters.

Much of what decides an edible product is settled before any chocolate is melted, in the question of what form the cannabinoid takes when it arrives. For a large part of the industry the answer is distillate, a word used daily on production floors and almost never explained outside them.

This is an account of what that word means: where the material starts, what the distillation THC passes through actually does to it, and why a manufacturer working at low doses cares so much about the result. It describes industrial processing carried out under licence. It is not a method, and nothing in it translates to a home setting.

Extraction comes first

The Cannabis Regulations define a cannabis extract as a substance produced by subjecting cannabis to extraction processing. Extraction pulls the oil-soluble compounds away from the bulk of the starting material. Cannabinoids are lipophilic, so they come with it, but so does a good deal else: waxes, fats, pigments and lighter aromatic compounds.

What leaves the extraction step is a crude oil. It is dark, it is variable, and its cannabinoid content shifts from one run to the next with the starting material and the conditions. That variability is the problem. A crude oil is a mixture whose composition has to be measured every time before it can be used for anything that carries a number on a label.

Short-path distillation

THC and the other major cannabinoids boil, at normal atmospheric pressure, at temperatures elevated enough that holding them there for long risks degrading the compounds you are trying to keep. Distillation works around that with vacuum. Lowering the pressure lowers the temperature at which a compound evaporates, so the separation can run hotter than room temperature but well short of what open air would demand.

In short-path distillation THC and the cannabinoids that travel with it are separated from the rest of the crude by volatility. The oil is heated under deep vacuum, the compounds that evaporate at a given temperature and pressure rise a very short distance to a cooled surface, condense, and are collected. The short path is the point of the design: less distance means less time spent hot, which means less opportunity for heat damage.

Industrial systems usually run this in stages. A peer-reviewed study of a two-stage wiped-film molecular distillation system, operated at 40 pascals, found that the first stage removed most of the terpenes and other lighter material, and the second stage refined the cannabinoid fraction itself. Wiped-film and short-path designs differ in their hardware, but they rely on the same principle: thin films, deep vacuum, short distances.

What it cannot do

Distillation sorts compounds by how readily they evaporate. Cannabinoids that evaporate at similar temperatures tend to come over together. The same study reported that low-pressure operation did not remove THC from a fraction rich in another cannabinoid, and its authors concluded the method was not suited to that task. The distillation THC goes through concentrates it. It does not, on its own, isolate it.

That limit is worth stating plainly because it explains why measurement never stops. A distillate is a refined mixture, not a single compound. However carefully the distillation THC went through was run, the only way to know what is in a given lot is to test that lot.

What distillate is

Distillate is the trade name for what comes out of the collection side: a thick, translucent, concentrated cannabinoid oil with most of the waxes, pigments and volatile compounds of the crude left behind. Legally it is still a cannabis extract. In manufacturing terms it is a raw material, valued less for any single property than for being predictable.

Predictable matters more than concentrated. A formulator does not need the strongest possible input. A formulator needs an input whose content is known, that behaves the same way in the next lot as in the last, and that can be weighed into a batch with confidence that the arithmetic on the label will hold. After distillation THC content can be measured against a far more stable baseline than a crude oil allows, and that is the property the whole downstream process depends on.

How that input is then carried into a finished product is a separate question, and it is where formulation choices begin. We have written about one of them, building the cannabinoid into the fat phase, in Cocoa butter cannabinoid extracts, explained.

Why a measured input matters at low doses

Under section 102.7 of the Cannabis Regulations, a package of edible cannabis may not contain more than 10 mg of THC. That cap sets the ceiling. How finely it is divided is a format decision, and the finer the division, the less room there is for error. A small deviation in a 10 mg total becomes a large one when the same quantity is split into ten pieces.

The Regulations also require testing. Section 91 requires each lot or batch to be tested for microbial and chemical contaminants, and section 92 requires phytocannabinoid testing using validated methods on a representative sample of each lot or batch. Those requirements apply to the finished product. They do not remove the need to know the input, because a product built on an input of uncertain content would be measured, found wrong and rejected, rather than made right.

That is why licensed edible manufacturers want a measured, tested input in the first place. A dependable label starts with a known quantity going into the batch and ends with laboratory confirmation coming out of it. Without distillation THC would reach the formulator inside a crude oil whose content moves with every extraction, and every number after that would inherit the uncertainty.
