How long does an edible last? What Health Canada publishes

The only timing figures worth repeating are the ones Health Canada publishes. Here they are, attributed, alongside the safety guidance that comes with them.

Most answers to this question online come from people describing their own experience. A manufacturer cannot do that, and should not. What a manufacturer can do is point to the figures the federal regulator publishes, quote them exactly, and explain why the waiting they describe is the part that matters most.

So this article answers how long does an edible last using only what Health Canada says, attributed in every sentence where a number appears. Why the digestive route is slower than inhalation is covered in how edibles are different, and is not repeated here.

The figures Health Canada publishes

Health Canada gives three timing figures for cannabis that is eaten or swallowed, in its guidance What you need to know if you choose to consume cannabis:

  • Health Canada advises that it can take 30 minutes to 2 hours after consumption for effects to begin.
  • Health Canada advises that it can take up to 4 hours for the full effects.
  • Health Canada advises that effects can last up to 12 hours, and that some residual effects could last up to 24 hours after use.

Those are ranges, not a schedule. Health Canada does not say an edible takes 30 minutes, or that it lasts 12 hours. It says the onset falls somewhere in a two-hour window, the full effect may not arrive for four, and the tail can run to a full day. Where any one person falls inside those ranges is not something a regulator, a retailer or a manufacturer can predict.

That is the honest answer, and it is a less tidy one than a single number. We would rather give the range with its source than a figure that sounds precise and is not.

Why the wait matters more than the number

The figure that carries the most practical weight is the four hours. Health Canada states plainly that consuming more within this time period can increase the risk of adverse effects. The delay is the hazard: a person who has had no response at the one-hour mark may conclude the first piece did nothing, when Health Canada's own range says it may simply not have arrived yet.

Health Canada's guidance on responsible use follows from that:

  • Start low. Health Canada advises that new consumers, or anyone wanting to minimize risk, look for a product containing 2.5 mg of THC or less.
  • Wait before taking more. In a 2023 public advisory, Health Canada advises consumers to start with a low amount of THC and wait for the effects before taking more.
  • Read the label. The same advisory tells consumers to read and understand the label, which shows the THC in each unit and in the whole package.
  • Do not combine. Health Canada advises avoiding consuming cannabis with nicotine, alcohol, other drugs or health products.

None of that is ours to improve on. The useful thing a producer can add is a reliable label. A declared figure only supports the start-low advice if the piece actually carries what the package says, and if the next piece matches the last one. That is a manufacturing obligation, and it is the reason dose consistency sits at the centre of our responsible consumption approach rather than at its edge.

Storage, children and emergencies

The long tail Health Canada describes has a second consequence. An edible package does not stop being a risk once the adult who bought it is finished with it. Edible cannabis looks like food, and some of the harm Health Canada has documented involves people who never chose to consume it at all.

In a 2020 advisory on accidental ingestion, Health Canada reported children being hospitalized after eating illegal edibles that resembled ordinary food products and had been kept in places like the fridge, without child-resistant packaging. Its storage guidance is specific:

  • Store all cannabis securely and out of reach of children, youth and pets, as Health Canada's consumer guidance puts it.
  • Keep cannabis in its original child-resistant packaging.

For an emergency, Health Canada is equally direct. If someone is having a serious medical emergency related to a cannabis product, Health Canada advises calling 911 or contacting your regional poison centre. For adverse effects, its advisory says to stop using the product and seek help from a poison centre or call 911. Health Canada also asks consumers to report suspected adverse reactions to it, and notes that the licence holder can be contacted as well.

Health Canada also publishes a number to call. On its page on cannabis poisonings in children, it advises: "If you think a child in your care has consumed cannabis, call 1-844-POISON-X (1-800-463-5060 in Quebec) for help." The same page adds: "If your child loses consciousness or has difficulty breathing, call 9-1-1."

What a manufacturer can tell you

We do not describe how anything feels, and we do not suggest when anything should be consumed. A licensed producer in Canada may not characterise effects, and individual responses vary in ways no producer can account for. The question of how long does an edible last belongs to Health Canada, and its answer is the one above.

What we can speak to is the half of the question that sits on the package. Every lot we produce is laboratory tested, and cannabinoid content is verified by laboratory analysis on every lot. Health Canada caps a package of edible cannabis at 10 mg of THC; how finely that is divided is a format decision, and each format tells you two figures: the total in the package and the amount in each piece. Those two figures are what Health Canada's start-low advice depends on, and making them accurate is the work we do in Thunder Bay, Ontario.
