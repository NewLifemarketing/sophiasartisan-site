Safe storage for cannabis at home, and why edibles need the most care

An edible outside its package looks like ordinary food. Health Canada's storage guidance is built around that fact, and it is set out here, attributed, with the figures we could verify.

An edible taken out of its package is, to anyone who cannot read the label, a piece of chocolate. That is the safety problem in a single sentence. It is why storage for cannabis matters more for edibles than for any other format, and why almost every line of Health Canada's guidance comes back to the package and the lock.

This article sets that guidance out, attributed, with the figures we were able to verify. It then covers the one part of storage that is a manufacturing question: what heat does to chocolate. Why the package itself is built the way it is, child-resistant and plain, is explained in why legal cannabis packaging looks the way it does, and is not repeated here.

What Health Canada advises

Health Canada's advice on storage for cannabis in the home is short, and it is specific. In its guidance on safe storage of cannabis, Health Canada advises the following:

  • Always store cannabis locked away and out of sight and reach of children and pets.
  • Use a secure cupboard with a strong childproof safety lock or latch, or consider a lockbox or safe.
  • Never leave cannabis unattended where children can access it.
  • Keep legal cannabis products in their original packaging, which keeps them more secure.
  • Always store edible cannabis separately from regular food and drinks.

For edibles made at home, Health Canada adds that they should be properly labelled, so it is clear they are not regular food. A legal product arrives labelled. A homemade one carries nothing unless someone adds it.

Two items on that list are the ones most often skipped, and both are about the package.

The first is keeping the original packaging. A legal package must be child-resistant, and its label states what the product is and how much THC it holds. Moving pieces into a jar, a tin or a sandwich bag removes both protections at once. Health Canada's 2020 advisory on accidental ingestion also notes that a tamper-evident seal stops doing its job once the package has been opened. After that, the child-resistant closure is the remaining barrier, and it only works when it is closed properly every time.

The second is keeping edibles apart from food. Health Canada's advisory on illegal copycat products warns that edibles can be mistaken for regular food or drink, especially once they are removed from their original packaging. Most homes keep chocolate in a kitchen cupboard or the fridge, which is where a child looks for something to eat. Good storage for cannabis puts it somewhere a child has no reason to look, and no means of opening.

Why edibles carry the particular risk

Health Canada's page on cannabis poisonings in children reports that hospitals have seen an increase in emergency room visits, that many cases involve children under 12, and that most of those involve children under five. It lists warning signs including vomiting, confusion, unusual drowsiness, slurred speech, trouble walking steadily and slowed breathing; its page gives the full list, including the most serious signs, and every parent should read it. Health Canada notes that severity depends on the child's size and how much THC was consumed.

Peer-reviewed data puts a number on it. A study published in JAMA Health Forum in 2023 examined poisoning hospitalizations among children aged nine and under in Ontario, Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec between 2015 and 2021. It recorded 581 hospitalizations for cannabis poisoning, and the children involved had a mean age of 3.6 years. In the provinces that permitted edible sales, cannabis accounted for 318 of every 1,000 child poisoning hospitalizations after edibles were permitted, close to one in three. Quebec, which restricted edible sales, did not show the same increase.

Health Canada caps an edible package at 10 mg of THC. That cap is set for adults. A low dose per piece is a format decision made for adults too, and it does not make any amount appropriate for a child. It is never a reason to store a product less carefully.

If a child eats an edible

Health Canada advises calling the poison centre at 1-844-POISON-X, or 1-800-463-5060 in Quebec. If the child loses consciousness or has trouble breathing, Health Canada advises calling 911 and saying you suspect cannabis. Its infosheet on storing cannabis safely carries the same numbers and describes accidental poisonings from edible cannabis as a serious risk to children and pets.

Keep the package to hand if you can. It is useful for the same reason the label exists: it says what the product is and how much THC each piece and the whole package contain. That is information nobody can reconstruct from a loose piece.

Keeping the chocolate itself stable

The last part of storage is a question about fat. Cocoa butter has a narrow melting range, and tempering sets it into one particular crystal form, which is explained in why chocolate is a delivery system. Warmth works against that structure.

A study in Food Science and Technology International stored dark chocolate at 4, 20, 25 and 30 °C for 30 days. Surface gloss declined over storage, and declined further at higher temperatures, which the authors related to how close the storage temperature sat to the melting range of cocoa fat. The pale surface known as fat bloom is the visible end of that process: the fat has moved and recrystallised.

So the integrity side of storage for cannabis chocolate is simple. Keep it in its sealed original package, at a steady room temperature, away from heat sources and direct sun. The fridge is cool, but it is also where food lives, and Health Canada's advice to keep edibles apart from food applies there as much as anywhere. A locked box in a cool cupboard meets both conditions. An open fridge shelf meets one.

Every lot we make is laboratory tested, and cannabinoid content is verified by laboratory analysis on every lot. That work ends when a package is sealed. What happens to it at home is governed by the guidance above, and the most important part of it has nothing to do with chocolate: locked, labelled, in its original package, and kept apart from food.
