HACCP cannabis requirements: the food-safety layer in edible manufacturing

The Cannabis Regulations never use the word HACCP. They do not need to. This is the hazard-control system an edible processor must run, where it comes from, and how it sits beside the cannabinoid rules.

The Cannabis Regulations never use the word HACCP. Search the consolidated text and it does not appear once. Yet any processor making edible cannabis in Canada is required to run a hazard analysis and critical control point system in all but name, written down, approved internally and kept on file. What is often described as HACCP cannabis compliance is, in law, the preventive control plan in Part 5 of the Regulations.

It is the food-safety layer of edible manufacturing. The cannabinoid rules ask whether a piece carries what its label says. This layer asks whether anything is in the piece that should not be.

What HACCP is, and where it comes from

Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point is a method of food safety built on controlling hazards at identified steps of production. Its principles were developed by the Codex Alimentarius Commission. The Codex Committee on Food Hygiene describes its core text, the General Principles of Food Hygiene (CXC 1-1969), as including the guidance on HACCP.

In Canada, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency's description of its Food Safety Enhancement Program sets out the seven principles in order:

  • identify and analyze hazards
  • determine the critical control points
  • establish critical limits
  • establish monitoring procedures
  • establish corrective action procedures
  • establish verification procedures
  • keep records

The CFIA describes critical limits as clear and measurable criteria that distinguish between what is acceptable and what is not. A critical control point is a step at which a control measure can be applied and is essential.

Where HACCP sits in the cannabis rules

Part 5 of the Cannabis Regulations brings the same structure in under its own vocabulary. It defines a critical control point as a step at which applying a control measure is essential to prevent or eliminate a biological, chemical or physical hazard that presents a risk of contamination, or to reduce it to an acceptable level. Section 88.93 then requires a processor that produces a cannabis extract or edible cannabis to identify and analyze those hazards, for the cannabis and for everything used as an ingredient, and to prevent, eliminate or reduce them using control measures shown by evidence to be effective.

Section 88.94 requires a written preventive control plan. Set its contents beside the CFIA list and the two line up item for item: the hazards identified, the control measures and the evidence they work, the critical control points, the critical limits for each, the procedures for monitoring against those limits, the corrective action procedures, the procedures for verifying that the plan achieves compliance, and the documents showing it has been implemented. Those implementation documents must be kept for at least two years.

Two features of the HACCP cannabis version are worth knowing. The plan has an internal approver named in law: section 88(1)(d) requires the quality assurance person to approve it before it is implemented. And nobody outside the licence holder signs it off in advance. Health Canada's guidance on preventive control plans states that it does not approve them and does not provide templates. Its page on creating a plan points processors to generic HACCP models and to guidance from bodies such as Codex Alimentarius.

Applying it to a chocolate line

Health Canada's planning guidance gives one example of each hazard class. Biological hazards are microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi and moulds; its example is Listeria surviving because a cooking time and temperature were inadequate. Chemical hazards can be naturally occurring or manufactured; its edible example is the over-addition of vitamins and minerals. Physical hazards are unwanted materials such as metal fragments, glass or wood splinters. Its examples of critical control points are a cooking step at a given time and temperature, and a metal detector at the end of the line. Those examples are the starting vocabulary of any HACCP cannabis plan for an edible, not its contents; the hazards a plan names have to come from analysis of the actual process.

A chocolate line adds one category every HACCP cannabis plan for this format has to take seriously: allergens. Milk is on Health Canada's list of priority food allergens, and milk chocolate contains it. Section 86(1)(e) of the Regulations requires equipment to be designed, maintained and arranged so that it protects cannabis and ingredients against allergen cross-contamination, and the sanitation program required by section 87 has to set out how that equipment is effectively cleaned.

The layout of the building is part of the plan too. Section 88.3 prohibits producing cannabis in a building where food that is to be sold is produced, packaged or labelled. Every food-safety control an edible processor relies on has to be built inside the licensed facility itself. Chocolate manufacturing has its own standards for temperature, humidity, sanitation and handling, and cannabis regulation is layered on top of that rather than replacing it; both apply at every step. The full sequence from incoming material to released lot is on our craft manufacturing page.

What HACCP cannabis planning does not cover

Health Canada's planning guidance does not frame cannabinoid content as a food-safety hazard. That question has its own rules. Section 90 requires cannabinoid testing on every lot, and section 97 sets how close each declared figure must be to the measured one. A processor runs both systems at once, and neither stands in for the other. A lot can be free of every listed contaminant and still fail on its declared figure, or sit exactly on its declared figure and fail on a hazard the plan missed.

That is why release is the last step, not a formality. Every lot is laboratory tested, and a quality assurance person, the same role that approved the preventive control plan, confirms the lot meets requirements before it leaves. How the testing half of that works is explained in how cannabis testing works before a legal product reaches a shelf. A HACCP cannabis plan is the other half: the record of what could go wrong at each step, and the evidence that it was controlled.

FAQ

Do the Cannabis Regulations require HACCP?

The Regulations never use the word. A processor making edible cannabis is still required to run the same system in all but name, as the preventive control plan under sections 88.93 and 88.94.

What are the seven principles of HACCP?

As the CFIA sets them out, identify and analyze hazards, determine the critical control points, establish critical limits, establish monitoring procedures, establish corrective action procedures, establish verification procedures, and keep records.

Does Health Canada approve a processor's preventive control plan?

No. Health Canada's guidance states that it does not approve plans and does not provide templates. Section 88(1)(d) requires the quality assurance person to approve the plan before it is implemented.

Does a preventive control plan cover cannabinoid content?

No. Health Canada's planning guidance does not frame cannabinoid content as a food-safety hazard. Section 90 requires cannabinoid testing on every lot and section 97 sets how close each declared figure must be, so a processor runs both systems at once.
