Can I take edibles on a plane in Canada? What the rules say

The answer depends on one question, which is whether the flight leaves Canada. Here is what the Cannabis Act, CATSA and the CBSA each say, and where each rule is published.

Two flights can leave the same Canadian terminal on the same morning and sit under completely different law. One lands in another province. The other lands outside Canada. For edible cannabis, that single difference decides almost everything, and most of the confusion around the question comes from treating the two as one.

So the honest answer to "can I take edibles on a plane in Canada" is a pair of answers. Within Canada, federal rules permit it inside the public possession limit. Across the border, in either direction, it is a criminal offence. Below is what each authority publishes, so you can check every line against the source yourself.

On a domestic flight: what CATSA and the Cannabis Act allow

The Canadian Air Transport Security Authority screens passengers and baggage at Canadian airports. Its published item list shows recreational cannabis as permitted in both carry-on and checked baggage. CATSA adds two conditions. Liquid and topical forms fall under the liquids, aerosols and gels rule and go in the one-litre clear bag with everything else of that kind. And CATSA states that passengers are responsible for knowing the laws and regulations that apply at their destination within Canada.

CATSA does not publish a quantity limit of its own. The federal limit on carrying cannabis in public is in the Cannabis Act. Under paragraph 8(1)(a), an adult 18 or older may not possess, in a public place, cannabis whose total amount is equivalent to more than 30 g of dried cannabis. Health Canada states the same figure on its possession-limit calculator: up to 30 grams of legal dried cannabis, or its equivalent, in public.

The word doing the work in both is "equivalent". An edible is not dried cannabis, so it has to be converted before it can be counted.

How an edible counts toward 30 grams

Subsection 2(4) of the Cannabis Act says the conversion is set by Schedule 3, and Schedule 3 is short. Each class of cannabis is given an amount deemed equal to 1 g of dried cannabis:

  • solids containing cannabis: 15 g
  • non-solids containing cannabis, other than beverages: 70 g
  • cannabis beverages: 570 g
  • cannabis concentrates: 0.25 g
  • fresh cannabis: 5 g

Chocolate is solid at room temperature, so a cannabis chocolate sits in the solids class. Running the arithmetic, which is ours and not a figure any authority publishes: 30 g of dried-cannabis equivalent corresponds to 450 g of solid edible product.

Two details are easy to miss. First, the schedule converts by the weight of the product, not by its THC content. A 450 g figure is a mass of chocolate, whatever milligram value is printed on each package. Second, section 8 counts "one or more classes" together. Edibles, a beverage and a concentrate carried at once are each converted and then added, and it is the total that must stay at or under 30 g.

In practice, nobody has to do this from memory. Health Canada's calculator refers to the dried cannabis equivalency amounts displayed on the label of products bought at retail. The figure on the package is the one to read. Separately, the Cannabis Regulations cap a container of edible cannabis at 10 mg of THC, so the quantity of THC in any single legal package is already fixed before the possession limit is ever reached.

Across the border, in either direction, it is illegal

This is the half of the question that has no limit to calculate. Subsection 11(1) of the Cannabis Act reads: "Unless authorized under this Act, the importation or exportation of cannabis is prohibited." Prosecuted on indictment, a contravention carries up to 14 years' imprisonment.

The Canada Border Services Agency applies that rule at every port of entry, airports included, and its published guidance closes the usual exits one by one:

  • It applies to cannabis in any form, including oils containing THC or CBD. An edible is not an exception.
  • It applies leaving Canada as well as arriving. The CBSA states plainly that you may not take cannabis out of the country.
  • Legal status elsewhere does not change it. Departing from, or flying to, a jurisdiction that has legalized cannabis makes no difference.
  • An authorization to use cannabis for medical purposes, from any country including Canada, does not permit carrying it across the border. Only a permit or exemption issued by Health Canada does.
  • Anyone arriving in Canada with cannabis in any form must declare it to the CBSA.

CATSA's own item page says the same thing from the screening side: flying outside of Canada with cannabis is illegal. Under the CBSA's penalty regime, undeclared cannabis is detained and a monetary penalty is issued, and depending on the seriousness of the case the agency may pursue criminal prosecution as well.

So if anyone asks you "can I take edibles on a plane in Canada" about a flight that crosses the border, the published answer is no, with no quantity at which it becomes yes.

Provincial rules, and why the package matters

Federal law sets the floor, not the whole picture. Health Canada notes that provinces and territories may set added restrictions, including raising the minimum age, lowering possession limits and restricting where cannabis may be used in public. The minimum age is 18 in Alberta, 21 in Quebec and 19 in the other provinces and territories. That is the reason CATSA points passengers to the law at their destination: a product that is lawful to possess where a flight departs is governed by the rules of the province where it lands.

On packaging, it is worth being exact. We found no federal rule that requires cannabis to be in its original package on a domestic flight. What Health Canada does publish is storage guidance: store all cannabis in its original child-resistant packaging. There is also a plain practical reason to follow it. The legal package is where the THC quantity per piece and per package, the equivalency figure and the product class are printed. Removed from it, a piece of chocolate carries none of that information. Our post on why legal cannabis packaging looks the way it does covers what each element of that label is required to say.
