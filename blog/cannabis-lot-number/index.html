What a cannabis lot number is, and why every package carries one

The lot number is the least read line on a legal package and the one the rest of the system depends on. This is what the Cannabis Regulations attach to it, and how it is used when something has to come back.

A cannabis lot number is the line on a legal package that tells a reader almost nothing and tells the manufacturer almost everything. It does not describe what is inside. It identifies which production run the package came from, and every regulated record about that run is filed under it.

What the label has to say

The requirement is in section 123 of the Cannabis Regulations. The label applied to any container in which a cannabis product is packaged must include the brand name, the recommended storage conditions, the packaging date and the lot number. The lot number must be preceded by one of four designations: "Lot number", "Lot no.", "Lot" or "(L)". Nothing in the rule depends on the class of product. A chocolate and every other cannabis product carry the same line.

It sits next to two dates that are easy to confuse with it. The packaging date says when the product was packaged, and section 123(2) allows the printed date to differ from the actual one by up to seven days. A durable life date, required on an edible whose durable life is 90 days or less, says how long it keeps. The cannabis lot number answers a different question entirely: not when, but which. As our education page puts it for retail staff, the lot number identifies the specific production run.

What is filed under the number

A lot is the unit the Regulations reason in. Most of the obligations that matter for an edible attach to it rather than to a product name or a brand.

  • Every lot or batch is tested before it can be sold, and a portion of the representative sample is kept for at least one year after the last sale of any part of the lot (sections 89 to 92, with the sampling and retention rules in section 92).
  • Every lot or batch must be approved by the quality assurance person before it is made available for sale (section 88(1)(e)).
  • For each lot or batch sold, the licence holder keeps a document demonstrating that the cannabis and every ingredient were produced, packaged, labelled, distributed, stored, sampled and tested as the Regulations require, and a document with the test results. Both are kept for at least two years after the last sale of any portion of that lot (section 231).
  • For each lot sold or distributed, the licence holder also keeps a document containing the information its recall system needs, for the same two years (section 235).

Read together, those sections make the lot number an index. The laboratory result, the quality release, the retained sample and the production record all point to the same run, and the number printed on the package is how anyone outside the building can name that run precisely. How the testing behind it works is set out in how cannabis testing works before a legal product reaches a shelf.

That is how we work. Every lot is laboratory tested, every lot is released by a quality assurance person before it ships, and cannabinoid inputs are sourced from licensed suppliers and verified on receipt.

How the number makes a recall precise

The lot number earns its place on the label when something has to come back. Section 46 requires every licence holder, other than an analytical laboratory, to maintain a system of control that permits the rapid and complete recall of every lot or batch it has sold or distributed. The same section requires a recall simulation at least once every 12 months, written up and kept for two years. The system is meant to be exercised, not only written down.

When a licence holder starts a voluntary recall, section 247 requires it to give the Minister a document first. That document identifies the product by brand name and gives the number of each lot or batch to be recalled. Where known, it also gives the number of any lot or batch of cannabis used to make the product. It sets out how much was sold or distributed, to how many persons, over what period, and how and when the recall will be carried out.

The input lot line is the part most people never see. A finished edible is made from cannabinoid inputs that carry lot numbers of their own. A problem traced to one input can therefore be followed forward to every finished lot that used it, and a problem in a finished lot can be followed back to its inputs. The cannabis lot number is one link in a chain that runs in both directions.

Retail is bound into the same chain. Under section 5.3 of the Regulations, a provincially authorized seller must not sell a cannabis product it knows is the subject of a voluntary recall begun for reasons of quality, or because the production or product requirements are not being met. The Minister can also order a recall under section 76 of the Cannabis Act where there are reasonable grounds to believe one is necessary to protect the public, and section 35 then prohibits selling or distributing the recalled cannabis. In each case, the lot number is how a store tells an affected package from an unaffected one sitting beside it.

Why the narrowness matters

Without lot numbers, the smallest unit anyone could recall would be a product name: every package ever made under it, whatever its history. With them, a recall can be drawn around exactly the runs that share a problem and no others. The records behind each cannabis lot number are what make that boundary defensible, because they show what each run was made from, how it was tested and who released it.

So the line that reads as the least interesting on the package is the one the rest of the label leans on. Every declared figure was measured on a lot, approved as a lot and recorded as a lot, and the lot number is the only part of that record the package carries with it.
