Why the nutrition facts table cannabis edibles carry is there

Edible cannabis is outside food law, yet its label carries a nutrition facts table. The Cannabis Regulations wrote their own version, with a fixed list of lines, a per-container basis and nothing permitted beyond it.

The nutrition facts table cannabis edibles carry looks familiar until you read the top line. There is no serving size. Every figure on it describes the whole container. That single difference says most of what there is to know about why the table exists and who wrote its rules.

Why an edible carries one at all

Edible cannabis is not governed by food law. The Cannabis Exemption (Food and Drugs Act) Regulations take cannabis handled under the Cannabis Act outside the Food and Drugs Act, and with it outside the Food and Drug Regulations that require a nutrition facts table on packaged food. On its own, that exemption would have left an edible with no nutrition facts requirement at all.

The Regulatory Impact Analysis Statement for the 2019 amendments explains what was done instead. The federal Task Force on Cannabis Legalization and Regulation had recommended that labelling requirements for food and beverage products also apply to edible cannabis. The amendments did not reconnect edibles to food law. They wrote a cannabis-specific table into the Cannabis Regulations, modelled on the standard format for packaged food, so that consumers could, in the statement's words, "make informed choices based on this information". The statement records that the proposal was widely supported in consultation.

The requirement itself is in section 132.18(1)(n): the label of an edible in discrete units must include a nutrition facts table that contains only the information set out in the table to section 132.22, and section 132.19 applies the same requirement to edibles that are not in discrete units. The word "only" matters, and it comes up again below.

What the table contains

Section 132.22 sets out the lines, the words each may use, the unit and the rounding. There are 15 items:

  • the container size, expressed "Per container" in grams or millilitres;
  • energy value, as calories;
  • fat, saturated fat, trans fat, and the sum of saturated and trans fat as a percentage of daily value;
  • cholesterol and sodium;
  • carbohydrate, fibre and sugars;
  • protein;
  • potassium, calcium and iron.

That is the energy value and the 12 core nutrients the impact statement describes. Some lines carry a percentage of daily value as well as an amount, and section 132.22(1) says that percentage must be calculated on the amount of the nutrient per immediate container. The rounding rules are written per line. Calories under 5 round to the nearest 1, from 5 to 50 to the nearest 5, and above 50 to the nearest 10. Fat under 0.5 g rounds to the nearest 0.1 g.

A nutrient that would be declared as zero may be left out, but only if the table says so in a fixed form: "Not a significant source of", followed by the nutrients omitted. And the layout is not the processor's choice. Section 132.22(3) requires the table to follow the applicable figure in Health Canada's Directory of Nutrition Facts Table Formats for Edible Cannabis, covering order, dimensions, spacing, case and bold type. The impact statement describes those formats as the food approach with adjustments for consistency with the rest of cannabis labelling, such as font size, font type and spacing.

The figures have to come from somewhere. The impact statement notes that processors must analyse the energy value and the fat, carbohydrate, protein and other nutrients in each product to fill the table accurately. That is the nutrition facts table cannabis law requires: a fixed set of measured values in a fixed format, and nothing more.

Why per container, and how it sits with the rest of the label

A food table states values per serving because a serving is how food is described. Edible cannabis is described per container throughout its label. The "Total THC" figure under section 132.18(1)(f) is for the whole container, and section 132.18(2) caps it at 10 mg. The nutrition facts table cannabis edibles carry uses the same basis, so every quantity on the package describes the same thing. How the THC figure and the per-piece figure relate is explained in what 10mg THC means on a legal Canadian edible.

The table also has a defined place in the hierarchy of the label. According to the impact statement, the health warning, the standardized cannabis symbol and the THC and CBD content must always appear on the exterior display surface. The nutrition facts table is among the items that may move to a peel-back or accordion panel, and section 132.27 lists it explicitly for edible cannabis. The cannabis information comes first; the food information can sit behind a fold.

It connects to the list of ingredients too. Under section 132.21(1)(g), sugars-based ingredients are grouped in that list after the word "Sugars", in order of their combined weight. The table gives the amount of sugars; the list shows where they came from.

The only place the numbers may appear

Here is where "only" earns its place. Section 132.29 prohibits any express or implied representation about the energy value or the amount of any nutrient, on the product, its package, its label or its panel. The impact statement gives "low-fat" as an example of a claim permitted on food and prohibited here. Section 104.13 extends the same rule to promotion. Edible cannabis may not be promoted by communicating information about its energy value or nutrients, with one exception: the required table may be reproduced, at smaller, larger or identical dimensions and spacing.

That is why this post describes the nutrition facts table cannabis regulations prescribe and gives no product's values. The table on the package is the one sanctioned statement of those numbers, and it is complete on its own terms. It is information for the reader of the label. It is never a selling point, and the Regulations are written so that it cannot become one. The constraints on the rest of the package are set out in why legal cannabis packaging looks the way it does.
